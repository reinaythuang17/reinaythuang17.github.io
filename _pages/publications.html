Working Papers

Bytes and Births: The effect of Broadband Internet on Infant Health

*Job Market Paper

How does greater accessibility to online health information affect infant health? As digital infrastructure expands, it has emerged as a critical determinant by enabling the dissemination of health information that either complements or substitutes for traditional care. Using the staggered rollout of broadband internet in the United States as a quasi-experimental variation, I employ recent advances in difference-in-differences design to linked data on broadband availability and birth certificates. I find that broadband availability significantly increases average birth weight and shifts the birth weight distribution, reducing low birth weight (<2,500g) while increasing high birth weight (>4,000g). Furthermore, while broadband decreases the aggregate fertility rate, these effects are heterogeneous across demographic groups. These results highlight internet as a meaningful role of early-life health and human capital formation. [2026 Feb Updated]

The Long Shadow of Typhoons: Prenatal Exposure and Adult Mental Health

(with Elaine M. Liu, Jin-Tan Liu, and Tzu-Yin Hazel Tseng) Draft available upon request

*Revise and Resubmit, Journal of Development Economics

This paper examines how poor in utero conditions caused by severe typhoons affect psychological well-being later in life. We exploit variation in the timing and location of severe typhoon landfalls. Our design compares individuals with higher expected fetal exposure in landfall counties to those with no such exposure. A one-unit increase in expected in utero exposure raises the likelihood of an adult mental illness diagnosis by 2.2 percentage points, or 11 percent relative to the mean. The incidence of mood disorders such as depression and the use of antidepressants has risen by more than 50 percent. The adverse effects are concentrated among women. In a fully interacted model, we reject equality of the male and female effects for our main outcomes. This gender gap is not driven by differential healthcare-seeking behavior. We discuss several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms that could generate a genuinely female-concentrated effect, including sex-specific fetal programming and mortality selection. [2026 Jun Updated]

Public Payment Mandates and Provider Supply

(with Lauren Hoehn-Velasco , Olanrewaju Yusuff)

*Revise and Resubmit, American Journal of Health Economics

[SSRN] [IZA]

Public insurance reimbursement policies shape the structure and reach of healthcare markets. In this study, we examine the 1980 federal Medicaid mandate requiring states to reimburse Certified Nurse-Midwives (CNMs), one of the first federal reforms targeting non-physician providers. We find the mandate increased midwife-attended deliveries by 1.1 percentage points in states that adopted the mandate before 1988. This 1.1 percentage point increase reflects a 69% rise in midwife deliveries, adding almost 1,300 midwife births annually per state by 1988. We also document a geographic expansion of midwife services into unserved areas and increased hospital employment. Our findings demonstrate that reimbursement mandates alter healthcare delivery by expanding provider use and reshaping the workforce. [2025 Dec Updated]

Full Day Kindergarten Mandates and Child Maltreatment

(with Bogyung Kim ) Draft available upon request

This study examines the effect of state-mandated full-day kindergarten (FDK) expansion on child maltreatment using NCANDS child-level data and NVSS mortality records. Leveraging the staggered rollout of FDK adoption across states with advanced difference-in-differences methods, we find that FDK policies lead to an increase of 3.5 physical abuse reports per 1,000 children—approximately 36% relative to the baseline mean. The absence of an effect on fatalities suggests that FDK primarily improves the detection of maltreatment rather than increasing its underlying incidence. Supporting this interpretation, educator-filed physical abuse reports rise by approximately 30%, reflecting enhanced detection capacity among teachers and school staff. Furthermore, the observed increases in reports from parents and neighbors imply that FDK may foster stronger community awareness and social support networks. Overall, this research provides new evidence on the broader benefits of early childhood education, particularly its role in strengthening child safety environments. [2026 Feb Updated]
